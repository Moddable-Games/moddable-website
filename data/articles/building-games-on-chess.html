Moddable Chess Engine v0.7.1 ships with a new developer guide that documents something we actually did — take a chess variant so ambitious it became its own standalone experience, and bring it home to the engine it was always meant to run on. Dungeon Chess v1.2.0 is the proof.

Dungeon Chess started as a chess mod — what happens when you replace the 8×8 grid with modular dungeon tiles, swap the 6 standard pieces for 24 fantasy units across four asymmetric factions, and add terrain hazards, special abilities, and XP drafting? The answer outgrew a single variant definition. It needed its own renderer, its own AI, its own move generation. Every piece type was hand-coded. Every terrain check was a hardcoded conditional. It worked, but it was a parallel universe — disconnected from the engine powering our other 70 variants.

The migration.

Over two weeks, we migrated Dungeon Chess from a standalone codebase onto MCE's consumer APIs. The game loop was replaced by createGameController. Fourteen unit handlers were rewritten as declarative templates using buildUnitHandler. The bespoke heuristic AI was replaced by MCE's negamax with alpha-beta pruning, quiescence search, and transposition tables.

The renderer — stone textures, flickering torchlight, floating embers, water caustics — now flows through MCE's tilePainter and pieceProvider hooks. The replay system uses createReplay. Capture animations use onAnimateMove.

The result: Dungeon Chess deleted over 500 lines of infrastructure code and came back to where it belongs — as the most complex variant in the MCE ecosystem, running on the same engine as Fischer Random and Atomic Chess, gaining every future improvement for free.

What v0.7 adds.

The consumer APIs that made this migration possible are now public and documented. MCE v0.7.1 exposes:

  • createGameController — full interaction loop with turn cycling, AI scheduling, and undo
  • createReplay — step-through replay with move annotations
  • renderBoard hooks — tilePainter, pieceProvider, legalMoveRenderer, afterRender, surroundRenderer
  • buildUnitHandler — declarative piece registration (movement, attacks, abilities in config)
  • Terrain predicates — global movement constraints without per-piece conditionals
  • Effect lifecycle — timed square effects with automatic expiry
  • Opening books — JSON-based book loading for AI opening play
  • positionKey — custom hash functions for non-standard piece ownership

Four guides.

The new Guides section on chess.moddable.games walks through every extension point with real implementations:

  1. Orda Chess — custom pieces with divergent movement and attack patterns
  2. Crazyhouse — action moves, hand management, non-standard move generation
  3. Poison Chess — the effects system, timed square states, conditional legality
  4. Dungeon Chess — building a complete game with terrain, capture interception, multi-step turns, and custom rendering

The Dungeon Chess guide isn't theoretical. It documents the exact migration path we took — every hook, every adapter, every decision point. If you're building a variant ambitious enough to need its own renderer and AI configuration, this is the blueprint.

The numbers.

MCE now supports 70 playable variants across six categories: classics, alternate win conditions, chaos variants, big boards, asymmetric, and multi-player. The AI handles all of them with variant-specific evaluation functions and configurable difficulty.

Dungeon Chess v1.2.0 — four factions, 24 units, terrain hazards, special abilities, and AI opponents — is a chess variant running on the same engine as Fischer Random. The most extreme mod in the library, proving the engine's ceiling.

"The best engine is one you can build things on that it was never designed for."

What's next.

MCE's consumer APIs are stable and versioned. We're building more variants on them — some will stay simple, others will push the engine as far as Dungeon Chess does. The guide pattern — documenting real migrations, not hypothetical tutorials — will continue as more ambitious mods ship.
