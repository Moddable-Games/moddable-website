What Nukes actually is.

Nukes is a cold-war territory control game on a modular hex map. No dice. No cards. No luck. Every outcome is the direct result of a decision you made.

The core mechanic: units are fluid. A single token is Infantry. Two tokens stacked together become Artillery. Three become Airborne. Four or more become a Base. The same physical pieces change behaviour based purely on how you group them:

  • Infantry floods through same-biome regions like water filling a container
  • Artillery jumps in straight lines over occupied hexes
  • Airborne slingshots exactly two regions, skipping what is between
  • Bases do not move, but convert isotopes and boost passing platoons

Win by destroying an opponent's city via nuclear strike, establishing a Base on their city, or being the last player with units on the board.

The hostage engine.

The second core mechanic: hostages are your engine. When you capture enemy units in combat, you hold them. Each turn, you must return 1-3 hostages to earn your moves. Every aggressive action fuels your next turn but simultaneously reinforces your opponent.

"What if every aggressive move also strengthened your opponent?"

Capture aggressively and you fuel your next few turns. But those returned hostages land back on the board as enemy reinforcements. Starve your opponent of hostages and they cannot move. But neither can you, because you have nothing to return. The equilibrium shifts constantly.

It is simple enough to explain in two sentences. It generates decisions that chess players and poker players both recognise as meaningful. The same design philosophy of emergent complexity from minimal rules that we explored in Conquering the Constraints of Chess.

The Catan conversion.

To play Nukes with your Catan set:

  1. Use the 19 hex tiles as your board (arrange in any configuration, not the standard Catan layout)
  2. Assign biome types to Catan terrain: Fields, Forest, Mountains, Desert, Clay Pits as Cities
  3. Use settlements or roads as your tokens (one colour per player, all pieces identical)
  4. Download the 4-page rulebook from the game page

No new components. No expansions. No purchases. The game you already own contains a second game inside it.

How it compares.

Where Nukes sits in the lineage of deterministic strategy:

  • Checkers: 2 players, 2 components, 2 biomes, 5x10^20 possibilities
  • Go: 2 players, 2 components, 1 biome, 10^800 possibilities
  • Chess: 2 players, 7 components, 2 biomes, 10^40 possibilities
  • Catan: 2-6 players, many components, 8 biomes, ~7 billion possibilities
  • Nukes: any players, 2 components, 5 biomes, infinite possibilities
