Should board games be open source? It's a slightly uncomfortable question for an industry built on intellectual property protection. But it is one of the most important questions facing tabletop gaming today.

Players already modify games extensively. They rebalance mechanics, create expansions, build digital versions, and share house rules across forums and Discord servers. The modification is happening regardless of whether publishers acknowledge it. The question is not if players will shape games. It is whether the industry structures itself to embrace that reality.

So what does "open-source" even mean for tabletop games?

In software, open-source means access to the code that makes a product work. For tabletop games, the equivalent involves a different set of layers:

  • Rules and mechanics — master source files for editing gameplay, available for forking and modification
  • Artwork and themes — community-owned visual identities with multiple skins available
  • Digital sandboxes — online engines for remote play and testing without physical components
  • Manufacturing access — transparent global partnerships for print-and-play and production

Each layer represents a different kind of openness. A publisher might open their rules but keep artwork proprietary. They might release digital assets but retain manufacturing exclusivity. Structured openness is not all-or-nothing. It is a spectrum.

The fear and the opportunity.

Designers, publishers, and artists have legitimate concerns. If anyone can modify and redistribute a game, what protects the creators who invested years of work? What prevents dilution of a brand? What happens to revenue?

But open-source models in other industries haven't eliminated value. They have shifted where value lives:

  1. Red Hat built a multi-billion-dollar business on free software
  2. WordPress powers 40% of the web while remaining open-source
  3. Linux runs nearly every server on Earth, maintained by a global community

The value moved from controlling the artifact to cultivating the ecosystem.

"Open-source models haven't eliminated value. They've shifted where value lives."

Enduring ecosystems over isolated hits.

The next generation of players has grown up with modded video games, remix culture, and collaborative platforms. They expect participation, not just consumption. A publisher who builds an ecosystem (tools, communities, shared standards) will outlast one who defends a single product behind walls.

Our licensing model Engines (MCE, Hexmaps, Rules) are MIT-licensed. Game content is CC BY-SA. Anyone can fork an engine. Anyone can build on our games with attribution. The infrastructure is open. The creative layer is protected but shareable.

This doesn't mean every game should be fully open on day one. But it does mean the industry needs new models: selective openness, tiered access, community governance, and revenue sharing.

What we believe.

At Moddable.Games, every mod we publish is freely available. Every ruleset is designed to be forked. Our engines are open-source. Our games are designed to be taken apart.

We don't believe this undermines the value of game design. We believe it amplifies it. The games that endure are the ones that communities invest in, and communities invest in things they can shape. As the ancients proved: the games that lasted 5,000 years had no IP protection. They survived because they were worth passing on.

The question is not whether players will shape games. It is whether we acknowledge that they already do.
