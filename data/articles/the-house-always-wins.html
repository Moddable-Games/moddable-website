Anyone who has rolled dice on a board has said at least once: "We don't play it that way." House rules are often dismissed as casual tweaks. Shortcuts for impatient players or patches for perceived flaws. Sometimes though, house rules do something much more interesting. Sometimes, they become canon.

No matter how hard some publishers might try, rulebooks are not sacred scripture. They are a snapshot of a designer's intent at a moment frozen in time. It is on the table where reality intervenes. Players discover strategies that stall momentum, or economic assumptions that unnecessarily inflate time. Some find eliminations discourage play or downtime that depletes a desire to replay. Yet in households in every nation all over the world, players adjust.

Rules that rewrote themselves.

What's fascinating is not that house rules exist; it's that some become widespread enough to redefine a game entirely:

  • En passant in Chess was introduced as a house rule over 500 years ago, standardised internationally 200 years later. Some people still believe it is merely a house rule and rage quit when used against them.
  • Free parking jackpot in Monopoly has no basis in the official rules, yet most players consider it canonical.
  • Auctioning unbought properties in Monopoly is an actual official rule that most players mistakenly believe is a house rule.
  • Homebrew classes in D&D are not fringe behaviour. Entire campaign settings and alternate systems were born at home tables before influencing official releases.

Why does this matter.

House rules reveal three important truths:

  1. Players are active participants in design, not passive consumers
  2. Communities will optimise for fun over purity
  3. The final version of the game may not be the one printed

If players are rewriting your rules, it implies they are deeply engaged with your system. They are doing exactly what we explore in Making Mods Matter: crossing the line from consumer to creator.

Versioning the tabletop.

What if publishers actively tracked and encouraged house rules? In a digital era, rules no longer need to be static. They can be versioned, tested, forked and merged.

Open-source solved this The software world has had version control, forking, and community contributions for decades. Tabletop games are next. Every mod we publish is versioned, documented, and open to pull requests.

The open-source model we are building treats house rules as first-class contributions. Our Moddable Rules engine versions every rulebook, tracks changes, and publishes diffs.
